\chapter*{Abstract}
\markboth{Abstract}{Abstract}

Electronic support measures systems passively intercept and analyze radar signals to identify the emitters present and how they operate.
After the receiver, each recording is typically reduced to a time-ordered stream of pulse descriptor words, in which pulses from several emitters are interleaved and each emitter may change its operating mode.
Operators need both groupings from that mixed list: which pulses belong to the same physical emitter, and which pulses share an operating mode.
Classical pipelines deinterleave first and then classify modes on the predicted single-emitter pulse trains.
Any deinterleaving error is passed on to mode recognition, since the pulse assignments are hard and carry no measure of confidence.
The two tasks also rely on overlapping pulse-level cues such as carrier frequency, pulse width, and pulse repetition pattern, which suggests that a representation shared between them, as in multi-task learning, may serve both better than two separately designed stages.
This thesis therefore treats the two partitions as a joint clustering problem.
A multi-task transformer encoder, composed of a shared trunk and two task-specific branches, maps a window of pulses to an emitter embedding and a mode embedding.
The branches are trained with two supervised contrastive losses that differ only in how positives are chosen: emitter identifiers versus mode labels.

At inference, density-based clustering (DBSCAN) is applied independently to the emitter and mode embeddings instead of assigning modes from a fixed catalogue, so both branches are trained and decoded in the same way.

We train and evaluate the encoder on a synthetic, fully labeled scenario corpus.
Each pulse carries a recording-local emitter identity and a mode from a catalogue of $19$ types shared across recordings.
Recordings are split into windows of $2000$ pulses, and scores are reported on $1169$ non-overlapping test windows.
We compare the encoder with DBSCAN on the raw pulse features, the joint objective with single-task training, and standard self-attention with two ways of encoding time of arrival in attention: rotary position encoding (RoPE) and a pairwise physical attention bias.

With standard self-attention, the joint encoder already recovers both partitions on most windows, with mean emitter adjusted Rand index (ARI) $0.948$ and mean mode ARI $0.985$, against $0.578$ and $0.811$ for DBSCAN on the raw features.
On emitters, however, it falls short of an encoder trained only for deinterleaving (ARI $0.999$).
Encoding time of arrival in attention, through RoPE or the pairwise bias, closes that gap: the joint encoder then reaches emitter ARI $1.000$ and mode ARI up to $0.992$, at or above every single-task encoder tested.
Held-out modulations and operational recordings are left to future work.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} transformer, deep clustering, electronic intelligence, electronic support measures, deinterleaving, radar emitter identification, mode recognition, supervised contrastive learning.
