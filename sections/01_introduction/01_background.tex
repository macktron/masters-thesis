\section{Background and Motivation}\label{sec:intro:background}

An electromagnetic wave carries electric and magnetic fields through space at a known speed.
A conducting body in its path scatters some of that energy back toward the source.
In 1904 Christian H\"ulsmeyer used that return to detect ships, decades before the method was named \gls{radar} and used to measure range \parencite{skolnik2001introduction}.
A transmitter that illuminates a scene also announces itself to anyone in range if they are capable of picking up the signal.

Passive \gls{esm} receivers do not transmit.
They record a time-ordered stream of \glspl{pdw} and feed \gls{elint} analysis: detecting radars, grouping pulses by emitter, and recognizing operating modes \parencite{wiley2006elint,skolnik2008radar}.
The listener now faces a mixture, not a single echo.
Radar and communications share a limited spectrum, and several emitters occupy it at once.

Modern radars vary carrier frequency, pulse width, amplitude, and \gls{pri} from burst to burst.
Fingerprints built on a stable pulse train then weaken, and parsers written for regular trains become less reliable.
The same emitter also switches mode, a recurrent control policy that maps a task such as volume search or track to a characteristic pulse regime.
Knowing the mode constrains how the waveform may evolve, and it can separate emitters that look similar at the type level.

Classical pipelines still parse these streams with rules written for regular trains.
When several agile emitters occupy the same window, the parser has to read context rather than pulses in isolation.
The supervision available in labelled training data is asymmetric: emitter identifiers are unique only within a recording, while operating modes come from a catalogue shared across recordings.

This thesis asks whether a transformer encoder, trained with clustering objectives, can map such streams to embeddings in which emitters and modes both form separable groups.
