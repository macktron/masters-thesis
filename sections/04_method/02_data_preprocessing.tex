\section{Data Representation and Preprocessing}\label{sec:method:data}

All training and evaluation data are synthetic because operational \gls{elint} recordings are scarce and sensitive.
A scenario simulator generates time-ordered \gls{pdw} recordings with varying numbers of emitters and operating modes \parencite{jonsson2023supcon}.
Each pulse contains the measured parameters and per-pulse labels defined in \cref{sec:method:formulation}.
The composition of the resulting corpus is reported in \cref{sec:exp:datasets}.

Preprocessing first standardizes carrier frequency, pulse width, and log-amplitude using statistics from the training split and rescales the two \gls{aoa} components.
Each pulse train is then divided into fixed-length windows.
Absolute \gls{toa} is finally min--max mapped inside each window, so that the timing feature seen by the encoder depends only on pulses that co-occur in the same attention context.
The encoder input is therefore the $d = 6$-dimensional vector of scaled carrier frequency, pulse width, log-amplitude, window-local \gls{toa}, and the two \gls{aoa} components.

No hand-crafted timing features are appended to this vector.
Preliminary experiments augmented each pulse with lagged timing features, namely its instantaneous $\Delta\mathrm{TOA}$ together with rolling means and standard deviations of $\Delta\mathrm{TOA}$ over several look-back widths, to expose the local \gls{pri} structure explicitly; this configuration consistently degraded both emitter and mode clustering relative to the plain \gls{pdw} input.
A plausible explanation is that such look-back statistics summarize only the pulses preceding each position and mix contributions from all interleaved emitters, so under the pulse-count imbalance of the corpus (\cref{sec:exp:datasets}) they are dominated by the densest emitters and carry little signal for rare ones, whereas unmasked self-attention (\cref{sec:method:arch}) can relate each pulse to every other pulse in the window and recover inter-arrival structure per emitter.
Letting the encoder learn timing structure implicitly also avoids committing to a fixed set of summary statistics, which keeps the representation free to adapt to interleaving patterns and \gls{pri} schedules that differ from those seen during training.

\subsection{Per-feature scaling}\label{sec:method:data:norm}

Standardization statistics are estimated across all pulse trains in the training split and applied unchanged to validation and test data.
Carrier frequency, pulse width, and log-amplitude are standardized per feature as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:standardize}
  \tilde{g}_n = \frac{g_n - \mu_g}{\sigma_g}.
\end{equation}
Here $\mu_g$ and $\sigma_g$ are the corresponding training-set mean and standard deviation, and amplitude is log-transformed to reduce its dynamic range.
The two \gls{aoa} components are mapped to $[0,1]$ by a fixed angular rescaling that does not depend on the training corpus,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:aoa_scale}
  \tilde{\theta}^{\mathrm{az}}_n = \frac{\theta^{\mathrm{az}}_n}{2\pi},
  \qquad
  \tilde{\theta}^{\mathrm{lat}}_n = \frac{\theta^{\mathrm{lat}}_n}{\pi}.
\end{equation}
Corpus-wide standardization of frequency, pulse width, and amplitude preserves absolute ranges that may distinguish operating modes across pulse trains.
Absolute \gls{toa} is left in physical units at this stage and is min--max mapped only after windowing (\cref{eq:method:minmax}).

\subsection{Windowing}\label{sec:method:data:window}

Standard self-attention has time and memory complexity $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$ for a window of $L$ pulses.
Because a complete pulse train may contain millions of pulses, the encoder processes fixed-length windows rather than entire trains.
The value of $L$ is reported with the experimental hyperparameters in \cref{tab:exp:hparams_main}.

Given a pulse train $\mat{X}=(\vect{x}_1,\ldots,\vect{x}_N)$ and stride $s$, window $w$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:windowing}
  \mat{X}^{(w)}
  =
  \bigl(\vect{x}_{1+(w-1)s},\ldots,\vect{x}_{L+(w-1)s}\bigr),
  \qquad
  w \in
  \left\{1,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{N-L}{s}\right\rfloor+1\right\}.
\end{equation}
The number of windows therefore varies with the pulse-train length.
Any trailing segment shorter than $L$ is dropped.

During training, choosing $s<L$ produces overlapping windows and therefore more training samples.
A smaller stride improves coverage of sparse emitters and modes and reduces sensitivity to window boundaries, but it also repeats more of the same \glspl{pdw}, increasing computation and correlation between samples.
The scenario-aware sampler in \cref{sec:method:training:sampler} allows the contrastive loss to use these repeated views.
Advancing the window by a small stride resembles streaming inference because outputs can be refreshed as new pulses arrive, although it is not online learning because the model parameters remain fixed.

Validation and test use $s=L$, yielding non-overlapping windows that are processed entirely offline.
Streaming inference and online model adaptation are left to future work (\cref{sec:concl:future}).

Each window retains the emitter label, mode label, and pulse-train identifier associated with every pulse.
The emitter and mode labels supervise their respective branches and provide evaluation targets.
The pulse-train identifier is not an encoder input or prediction target; it is used to keep train-local emitter identifiers distinct and to form scenario-aware mini-batches.

After windowing, absolute \gls{toa} is min--max mapped to $[0,1]$ using only the $L$ pulses inside the current window:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:minmax}
  \tilde{t}^{(w)}_i
  =
  \frac{t^{(w)}_i - t^{(w)}_{\min}}{t^{(w)}_{\max} - t^{(w)}_{\min}},
\end{equation}
where $t^{(w)}_{\min}$ and $t^{(w)}_{\max}$ are the minimum and maximum physical \gls{toa} among the pulses of window $w$, and $i$ indexes positions within that window.
The encoder never observes pulses outside the window it processes, so recording-level extrema would inject timing information unavailable inside the attention context (and unavailable to a streaming receiver that buffers only the current window).
Per-window mapping keeps the \gls{toa} channel self-contained; when training uses $s < L$, the same physical pulse may receive different normalized values in overlapping windows, which is the intended behaviour for a window-local feature.
