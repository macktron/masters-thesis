\section{Data Representation and Preprocessing}\label{sec:method:data}

All training and evaluation data are synthetic because operational \gls{elint} recordings are scarce and sensitive.
A scenario simulator generates time-ordered \gls{pdw} recordings with varying numbers of emitters and operating modes \parencite{jonsson2023supcon}.
Each pulse contains the measured parameters and per-pulse labels defined in \cref{sec:method:formulation}.
The composition of the resulting corpus is reported in \cref{sec:exp:datasets}.

Preprocessing first standardizes carrier frequency, pulse width, and log-amplitude using statistics from the training split and converts the measured \gls{aoa} into Euclidean direction components.
Each recording is then divided into fixed-length windows.
Absolute \gls{toa} is finally min--max mapped inside each window, so that the timing feature seen by the encoder depends only on pulses that co-occur in the same attention context, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting to scenario-specific timing characteristics and providing a more realistic representation of the information available at inference time.
The encoder input is therefore the $d = 7$-dimensional vector of scaled carrier frequency, pulse width, log-amplitude, window-local \gls{toa}, and the three incidence-direction components.

No hand-crafted timing features are appended to this vector.
Preliminary experiments augmented each pulse with lagged timing features, namely its instantaneous $\Delta\mathrm{TOA}$ together with rolling means and standard deviations of $\Delta\mathrm{TOA}$ over several look-back widths, to expose the local \gls{pri} structure explicitly; this configuration consistently degraded both emitter and mode clustering relative to the plain \gls{pdw} input.
A plausible explanation is that such look-back statistics summarize only the pulses preceding each position and mix contributions from all interleaved emitters, so under the pulse-count imbalance of the corpus (\cref{sec:exp:datasets}) they are dominated by the densest emitters and carry little signal for rare ones, whereas unmasked self-attention (\cref{sec:method:arch}) can relate each pulse to every other pulse in the window and recover inter-arrival structure per emitter.
Letting the encoder learn timing structure implicitly also avoids committing to a fixed set of summary statistics, which keeps the representation free to adapt to interleaving patterns and \gls{pri} schedules that differ from those seen during training.

\subsection{Per-feature scaling}\label{sec:method:data:norm}

Standardization statistics are estimated across all recordings in the training split and applied unchanged to validation and test data.
Carrier frequency, pulse width, and log-amplitude are standardized per feature as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:standardize}
  \tilde{g}_n = \frac{g_n - \mu_g}{\sigma_g}.
\end{equation}
Here $\mu_g$ and $\sigma_g$ are the corresponding training-set mean and standard deviation, and amplitude is log-transformed to reduce its dynamic range.
The measured \gls{aoa} consists of an azimuth $\theta^{\mathrm{az}}_n$ and a latitude $\theta^{\mathrm{lat}}_n$, which together specify the incidence direction of the pulse on the unit sphere.
Rather than feeding these angles to the encoder directly, each pair is converted to the Euclidean unit direction vector
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:aoa_cart}
  \vect{u}_n
  =
  \bigl(u^{x}_n,\, u^{y}_n,\, u^{z}_n\bigr)^{\top}
  =
  \bigl(
    \cos\theta^{\mathrm{lat}}_n \cos\theta^{\mathrm{az}}_n,\;
    \cos\theta^{\mathrm{lat}}_n \sin\theta^{\mathrm{az}}_n,\;
    \sin\theta^{\mathrm{lat}}_n
  \bigr)^{\top},
\end{equation}
whose components lie in $[-1,1]$ and are mapped to $[0,1]$ by the fixed affine rescaling
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:aoa_scale}
  \tilde{u}^{c}_n = \frac{u^{c}_n + 1}{2},
  \qquad
  c \in \{x, y, z\}.
\end{equation}
Neither map depends on the training corpus.

The Cartesian representation avoids the wrap-around discontinuity of a rescaled angle, which places azimuths near $0$ and $2\pi$ at opposite ends of the feature range although they describe nearly the same direction.
This matters especially for the attention variants of \cref{sec:method:attention}, whose pairwise bias and rotary encoding both act on coordinate differences: differences of direction components respect the geometry of the sphere, whereas differences of raw angles do not.

Corpus-wide standardization of frequency, pulse width, and amplitude preserves absolute ranges that may distinguish operating modes across recordings.
Absolute \gls{toa} is left in physical units at this stage and is min--max mapped only after windowing (\cref{eq:method:minmax}).

\subsection{Windowing}\label{sec:method:data:window}

Standard self-attention has time and memory complexity $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$ for a window of $L$ pulses.
Because a complete recording may contain millions of pulses, the encoder processes fixed-length windows rather than entire recordings.
The value of $L$ is reported with the experimental hyperparameters in \cref{tab:exp:hparams_main}.

Given a recording $\mat{X}=(\vect{x}_1,\ldots,\vect{x}_N)$ and stride $\delta$, window $w$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:windowing}
  \mat{X}^{(w)}
  =
  \bigl(\vect{x}_{1+(w-1)\delta},\ldots,\vect{x}_{L+(w-1)\delta}\bigr),
  \qquad
  w \in
  \left\{1,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{N-L}{\delta}\right\rfloor+1\right\}.
\end{equation}
The superscript $(w)$ indexes windows within one recording, whereas $(s)$ indexes recordings and is suppressed here.
The number of windows therefore varies with the recording length.
Any trailing segment shorter than $L$ is dropped.

During training, choosing $\delta<L$ produces overlapping windows and therefore more training samples.
A smaller stride improves coverage of sparse emitters and modes and reduces sensitivity to window boundaries, but it also repeats more of the same \glspl{pdw}, increasing computation and correlation between samples.
The scenario-aware sampler in \cref{sec:method:training:sampler} allows the contrastive loss to use these repeated views.
Advancing the window by a small stride resembles streaming inference because outputs can be refreshed as new pulses arrive, although it is not online learning because the model parameters remain fixed.

Validation and test use $\delta=L$, yielding non-overlapping windows that are processed entirely offline.
Streaming inference and online model adaptation are left to future work (\cref{sec:concl:future}).

Each window retains the emitter label, mode label, and recording identifier associated with every pulse.
The emitter and mode labels supervise their respective branches and provide evaluation targets. This makes the model and also evaluation dependent on the selected window length L, why a fixed L is used in this thesis.

The recording identifier is not an encoder input or prediction target; it is used to keep recording-local emitter identifiers distinct and to form scenario-aware mini-batches.

After windowing, absolute \gls{toa} is min--max mapped to $[0,1]$ using only the $L$ pulses inside the current window:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:minmax}
  \tilde{t}^{(w)}_i
  =
  \frac{t^{(w)}_i - t^{(w)}_{\min}}{t^{(w)}_{\max} - t^{(w)}_{\min}},
\end{equation}
where $t^{(w)}_{\min}$ and $t^{(w)}_{\max}$ are the minimum and maximum \gls{toa} among the pulses of window $w$, and $i$ indexes positions within that window.
The encoder never observes pulses outside the window it processes, so recording-level extrema would inject timing information unavailable inside the attention context (and unavailable to a streaming receiver that buffers only the current window).
Per-window mapping keeps the \gls{toa} channel self-contained; when training uses $\delta < L$, the same physical pulse may receive different normalized values in overlapping windows, which is the intended behaviour for a window-local feature.
