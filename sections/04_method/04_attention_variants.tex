\section{Attention Variants}\label{sec:method:attention}

The encoder of \cref{sec:method:arch} uses standard self-attention, which treats a window as an unordered set of tokens and lets every pulse attend to every other pulse purely through learned query--key compatibility.
The only ordering or physical-relationship cue available to it is whatever the input features themselves carry, in particular the \gls{toa} entry of each \gls{pdw}.
A radar pulse train, however, has strong pairwise structure: two pulses that are close in time, share a carrier frequency, or have a similar pulse width are far more likely to originate from the same emitter or mode than an arbitrary pair.
This section describes three variants of the attention mechanism that make such structure explicit. Each is evaluated as a drop-in replacement for the \gls{mha} sub-layers of \cref{sec:method:arch}; their relative merit is reported in \cref{ch:experiments}.

Throughout, we write the attention logits of a single head over a window of $L$ pulses as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:attn_base}
  \mat{A} = \frac{\mat{Q}\mat{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}},
  \qquad
  A_{ij} = \frac{\vect{q}_i^{\top}\vect{k}_j}{\sqrt{d_k}},
\end{equation}
where $\vect{q}_i$ and $\vect{k}_j$ are the query and key of pulses $i$ and $j$ and $d_k$ is the per-head dimension.
The attention weights are the row-wise softmax of $\mat{A}$, which then mixes the value vectors.

\subsection*{Basic attention bias}

The first variant adds a handcrafted pairwise bias to the logits,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:attn_bias}
  A_{ij} = \frac{\vect{q}_i^{\top}\vect{k}_j}{\sqrt{d_k}} + B_{ij},
\end{equation}
where $B_{ij}$ is a fixed function of the physical relationship between pulses $i$ and $j$, such as their time separation $|t_i - t_j|$, carrier-frequency difference $|f_i - f_j|$, or pulse-width difference.
Designing $B$ so that physically related pairs receive a larger value pushes attention towards them before any learning takes place, injecting radar-domain knowledge directly into the mechanism.
This mirrors the relative-position and content-independent biases used in language and graph transformers \parencite{shaw2018relative,ying2021graphormer}, specialised here to pulse parameters rather than token indices.

\subsection*{MLP attention bias}

Choosing $B$ by hand fixes in advance which relationships matter and how strongly.
The second variant instead learns the bias: for each pulse pair we form a vector of pairwise features $\vect{\phi}_{ij}$ (the same physical differences as above, and any others worth including) and pass it through a small shared \gls{mlp},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:attn_mlp_bias}
  B_{ij} = \mathrm{MLP}(\vect{\phi}_{ij}),
\end{equation}
which is then inserted into \cref{eq:method:attn_bias} exactly as before.
The network is free to discover which pulse relationships should increase or decrease attention and by how much, rather than committing to a hand-tuned rule.
This generalises the fixed bias of \cref{eq:method:attn_bias} and is the edge-feature analogue of attention used in graph transformers \parencite{ying2021graphormer}.

\subsection*{Rotary positional encoding}

The two bias variants encode \emph{which} pulses are related; the third encodes \emph{where} a pulse sits in the sequence.
\Gls{rope} \parencite{su2024roformer} injects position not as an additive signal but as a phase: it rotates each query and key by an angle proportional to its position before the dot product of \cref{eq:method:attn_base} is taken, so that the per-head dimension $d_k$ (assumed even) is treated as $d_k/2$ independent two-dimensional planes.
On each plane a rotation is the orthogonal matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope_2d}
  \mat{\Omega}(\phi) =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \cos\phi & -\sin\phi \\
    \sin\phi & \phantom{-}\cos\phi
  \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{\Omega}(\phi)^{\top}\mat{\Omega}(\psi) = \mat{\Omega}(\psi - \phi),
\end{equation}
the latter identity expressing that composing two planar rotations yields a single rotation by their angular difference.
For a pulse at position $i$, \gls{rope} assembles these planar rotations into a block-diagonal matrix whose $m$-th block rotates the $m$-th plane by $i\theta_m$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope_block}
  \mat{R}_i = \diag\!\bigl(\mat{\Omega}(i\theta_1),\,\mat{\Omega}(i\theta_2),\,\ldots,\,\mat{\Omega}(i\theta_{d_k/2})\bigr),
  \qquad
  \theta_m = b^{-2(m-1)/d_k},\quad m = 1,\ldots,\tfrac{d_k}{2},
\end{equation}
with a fixed base $b$ (we use $b = 10^4$, following \textcite{su2024roformer}). The geometric progression of angular frequencies $\theta_m$ mirrors the wavelengths of the sinusoidal encoding of \textcite{vaswani2017attention}: low-index planes rotate quickly and resolve fine separations, high-index planes rotate slowly and capture coarse ones.
Each $\mat{R}_i$ is orthogonal, so $\mat{R}_i^{\top} = \mat{R}_i^{-1} = \mat{R}_{-i}$ and, by \cref{eq:method:rope_2d} applied block-wise, $\mat{R}_i^{\top}\mat{R}_j = \mat{R}_{j-i}$.

Applying the rotation to the query and key and forming their inner product therefore yields a logit that depends only on the relative separation $j - i$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope}
  \tilde{\vect{q}}_i = \mat{R}_i\,\vect{q}_i,
  \qquad
  \tilde{\vect{k}}_j = \mat{R}_j\,\vect{k}_j,
  \qquad
  \tilde{\vect{q}}_i^{\top}\tilde{\vect{k}}_j
    = \vect{q}_i^{\top}\mat{R}_i^{\top}\mat{R}_j\,\vect{k}_j
    = \vect{q}_i^{\top}\mat{R}_{j-i}\,\vect{k}_j,
\end{equation}
even though each token is rotated by its absolute position. \Cref{fig:method:rope} illustrates this on a single plane: incrementing the position fans a vector around the circle by a fixed angle (left), while the phase enclosed between a rotated query and key—and hence their dot product—is set by the position gap alone (right).

This supplies an explicit notion of pulse ordering and separation, complementing rather than replacing the bias terms: the attention bias encodes radar-specific pulse relationships, while \gls{rope} encodes temporal ordering.
Because the rotation angle $i\theta_m$ is well defined for any real argument, the integer index $i$ can be replaced by a continuous coordinate such as the normalised \gls{toa}, in which case the relative phase $\mat{R}_{j-i}$ becomes a function of the physical time gap between two pulses rather than of their ordinal distance—a natural fit for the irregularly sampled pulse trains of \cref{sec:method:data}.
It also revisits the choice of \cref{sec:method:arch} to omit a positional encoding and rely on the \gls{toa} feature alone, testing whether explicit relative position adds information beyond it.
