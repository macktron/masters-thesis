\section{Attention Variants}\label{sec:method:attention}

The encoder of \cref{sec:method:arch} uses standard self-attention, which treats a window as an unordered set of tokens and lets every pulse attend to every other pulse purely through learned query--key compatibility.
The only ordering cue available to it is whatever the input features themselves carry, in particular the \gls{toa}.
A radar recording, however, has strong pairwise structure: two pulses that are close in time, share a carrier frequency, have a similar pulse width, or arrive from a similar direction, are far more likely to originate from the same emitter or mode than an arbitrary pair.
This section describes two variants of the attention mechanism that make such structure explicit.
They can be used alone or together as drop-in modifications of the \gls{mha} sub-layers of \cref{sec:method:arch}; all three configurations are evaluated in \cref{ch:experiments}.

Throughout, we write the attention logits of a single head over a window of $L$ pulses as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:attn_base}
  \mat{A} = \frac{\mat{Q}\mat{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}},
  \qquad
  A_{ij} = \frac{\vect{q}_i^{\top}\vect{k}_j}{\sqrt{d_k}},
\end{equation}
where $\vect{q}_i$ and $\vect{k}_j$ are the query and key of pulses $i$ and $j$ and $d_k$ is the per-head dimension.
The attention weights are the row-wise softmax of $\mat{A}$, which then mixes the value vectors.

\subsection*{Physical attention bias}

The first variant adds a pairwise bias to the attention logits, computed from the physical parameters of each pulse pair.
The bias uses two coordinates: the window-local \gls{toa} $t_n$ and the azimuth $\theta^{\mathrm{az}}_n$ of \cref{eq:method:aoa_cart}.
For \gls{toa} the pairwise feature is the absolute difference $B^{(t)}_{ij} = |t_i - t_j|$.
For azimuth, a plain absolute difference would treat bearings near $0$ and $2\pi$ as far apart although they describe nearly the same direction; the circular distance
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:circ_az}
  B^{(\theta)}_{ij}
  =
  \frac{1}{\pi}\,
  \min\bigl(
    \lvert\theta^{\mathrm{az}}_i - \theta^{\mathrm{az}}_j\rvert,\,
    2\pi - \lvert\theta^{\mathrm{az}}_i - \theta^{\mathrm{az}}_j\rvert
  \bigr)
  \in [0,1]
\end{equation}
takes the shorter arc on the circle and normalises by $\pi$ so that both bias features lie in $[0,1]$.
Each feature is added to the logits with a learnable scalar coefficient,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:attn_bias}
  A_{ij}
  =
  \frac{\vect{q}_i^{\top}\vect{k}_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}
  + \lambda_t\, B^{(t)}_{ij}
  + \lambda_{\theta}\, B^{(\theta)}_{ij}.
\end{equation}
Each coefficient is learned jointly with the rest of the network and belongs to a single head of a single attention sub-layer, so every head in every encoder block---throughout the shared trunk and both branches---carries its own $(\lambda_t,\lambda_{\theta})$.
This adds only about \(192\) scalar parameters in total; the pairwise delta matrices themselves scale quadratically in the window length, as the attention mechanism already does.
Coefficients may be negative, and all is initialised to a small negative value so that, in line with the intended inductive bias, a larger coordinate difference initially decreases attention between the corresponding pulses.
Because the coefficients are learned independently in each part of the network, the branches can weight the cues differently.
The deinterleaving branch is expected to rely more on azimuth, since pulses from one emitter usually arrive from a nearly constant bearing.
The shared trunk and the mode branch should instead suppress that cue, because an operating mode must be recognised independently of viewing geometry.
Whether this allocation emerges is examined in \cref{ch:experiments}.
The bias follows the same idea as additive pairwise attention biases in relative-position and graph Transformers \parencite{shaw2018relative,ying2021graphormer}, but uses continuous pulse parameters instead of discrete token or path distances.

\subsection*{Rotary positional encoding}

The bias variant adjusts the logits after the query--key product is formed; the second variant instead builds positional information into the queries and keys themselves.
Notation below is for a single attention head.
\Gls{mha} with $h$ heads uses $h$ independent projection pairs; each head maps the token representation $\vect{h}_i \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}$ to per-head queries and keys of dimension $d_k = d_{\mathrm{model}}/h$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:qk_proj}
  \vect{q}_i = \mat{W}_q\vect{h}_i,
  \qquad
  \vect{k}_i = \mat{W}_k\vect{h}_i,
\end{equation}
with learnable $\mat{W}_q, \mat{W}_k \in \R^{d_k \times d_{\mathrm{model}}}$ shared across positions within that head.
The $h$ head outputs are concatenated and mixed as in standard \gls{mha}.
In the vanilla self-attention of \cref{eq:method:attn_base}, the attention logit of one head is then simply the inner product
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:attn_dot}
  \bigl\langle \vect{q}_i,\, \vect{k}_j \bigr\rangle
  = \vect{q}_i^{\top}\vect{k}_j
  = (\mat{W}_q\vect{h}_i)^{\top}(\mat{W}_k\vect{h}_j).
\end{equation}
\Gls{rope} leaves these projections unchanged and rotates the resulting vectors by a phase that depends on a scalar coordinate $p$ attached to each pulse.
Following \textcite{su2024roformer}, the query and key become functions $f_q(\vect{h}_i, p_i)$ and $f_k(\vect{h}_j, p_j)$ of both the hidden representation and that coordinate, and their inner product is required to depend on the coordinates only through their difference,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope_goal}
  \bigl\langle f_q(\vect{h}_i, p_i),\, f_k(\vect{h}_j, p_j) \bigr\rangle
  = g(\vect{h}_i, \vect{h}_j, p_j - p_i).
\end{equation}
In the original formulation the coordinate is the token index, $p_i = i$.
We instead attach physical coordinates rather than token indices, so that $p_j - p_i$ is a time gap or a difference of direction features rather than an ordinal distance; this suits the irregularly sampled recordings of \cref{sec:method:data}, where consecutive indices may be microseconds or milliseconds apart.

\Gls{rope} satisfies \cref{eq:method:rope_goal} by encoding that scalar as a phase applied to the whole head.
The $d_k$ dimensions of a head (assumed even) are grouped into $d_k/2$ two-dimensional planes, and the $m$-th plane is rotated by the angle $\gamma p\omega_m$, where $\omega_m$ is a per-plane angular frequency and $\gamma > 0$ a coordinate scale, both specified below.
Each plane uses the planar rotation matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope_2d}
  \mat{\Omega}(\phi) =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \cos\phi & -\sin\phi \\
    \sin\phi & \phantom{-}\cos\phi
  \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Collecting the per-plane rotations into a single block-diagonal matrix of size $d_k \times d_k$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope_block}
  \mat{R}(p)
  = \diag\!\bigl(
      \mat{\Omega}(\gamma p\omega_1),\,
      \mat{\Omega}(\gamma p\omega_2),\,
      \ldots,\,
      \mat{\Omega}(\gamma p\omega_{d_k/2})
    \bigr),
\end{equation}
or written out entry-wise,
\[
  \mat{R}(p) =
  {\scriptsize
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \cos\gamma p\omega_1 & -\sin\gamma p\omega_1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
    \sin\gamma p\omega_1 & \phantom{-}\cos\gamma p\omega_1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & \cos\gamma p\omega_2 & -\sin\gamma p\omega_2 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & \sin\gamma p\omega_2 & \phantom{-}\cos\gamma p\omega_2 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & \cos\gamma p\omega_{d_k/2} & -\sin\gamma p\omega_{d_k/2} \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & \sin\gamma p\omega_{d_k/2} & \phantom{-}\cos\gamma p\omega_{d_k/2}
  \end{bmatrix}.}
\]
The angular frequencies $\omega_m = b^{-2(m-1)/d_k}$, with a fixed base $b$ (we use $b = 10^4$, following \textcite{su2024roformer}), form a geometric progression that mirrors the wavelengths of the sinusoidal encoding of \textcite{vaswani2017attention}: fast-rotating planes resolve fine coordinate differences, slow-rotating planes capture coarse ones.
The scale $\gamma$ multiplies every frequency of the ladder uniformly and therefore sets how much relative phase one unit of coordinate difference produces.
In the original formulation no such factor is needed, because token indices advance in steps of exactly one; our coordinates are normalised quantities in $[0,1]$ whose typical differences depend on the quantity, so $\gamma$ is treated as a hyperparameter of the attached coordinate.
The values used for each coordinate are reported in \cref{ch:experiments}; the dependence of $\mat{R}$ on $\gamma$ is left implicit in the notation.
Applying $\mat{R}(p)$ after the projections of \cref{eq:method:qk_proj} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope_map}
  f_q(\vect{h}_i, p_i) = \mat{R}(p_i)\,\vect{q}_i = \mat{R}(p_i)\,\mat{W}_q\vect{h}_i,
  \qquad
  f_k(\vect{h}_j, p_j) = \mat{R}(p_j)\,\vect{k}_j = \mat{R}(p_j)\,\mat{W}_k\vect{h}_j,
\end{equation}
written $\tilde{\vect{q}}_i$ and $\tilde{\vect{k}}_j$ below; without the rotation, \cref{eq:method:rope_map} reduces to the vanilla case of \cref{eq:method:attn_dot}.

That this construction meets the design goal follows from one property of planar rotations: composing two of them subtracts their angles, $\mat{\Omega}(\phi)^{\top}\mat{\Omega}(\psi) = \mat{\Omega}(\psi - \phi)$, hence block-wise $\mat{R}(p_i)^{\top}\mat{R}(p_j) = \mat{R}(p_j - p_i)$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope}
  \bigl\langle \tilde{\vect{q}}_i,\, \tilde{\vect{k}}_j \bigr\rangle
    = \vect{q}_i^{\top}\,\mat{R}(p_j - p_i)\,\vect{k}_j
    = (\mat{W}_q\vect{h}_i)^{\top}\,\mat{R}(p_j - p_i)\,(\mat{W}_k\vect{h}_j),
\end{equation}
which is exactly the form required by \cref{eq:method:rope_goal}: each pulse is rotated by its absolute coordinate, yet the logit sees only the difference.
Setting $\mat{R}(p) = \mat{I}$ recovers the content-only inner product of \cref{eq:method:attn_dot}.
\Cref{fig:method:rope} illustrates this on a single plane: increasing the coordinate fans a vector around the circle at a fixed rate (left), while the phase enclosed between a rotated query and key---and hence their dot product---is set by the coordinate gap alone (right).

The encoder of \cref{sec:method:arch} uses this construction everywhere.
The shared trunk and the mode branch apply a single coordinate (the \gls{toa}).
The deinterleaving branch instead splits each head across two continuous functions of azimuth, as detailed below.

\subsubsection*{Trunk and mode branch}

Every head in the shared trunk and in the mode branch rotates by the window-local \gls{toa},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope_toa}
  \tilde{\vect{q}}_n = \mat{R}(t_n)\,\vect{q}_n,
  \qquad
  \tilde{\vect{k}}_n = \mat{R}(t_n)\,\vect{k}_n,
\end{equation}
with scale $\gamma_t$ in $\mat{R}$.
Arrival direction is excluded: an operating mode is characterised by the intrinsic temporal pattern of the emission and must be recognised regardless of viewing geometry, so rotating these layers by direction would inject nuisance variation into an embedding that should be geometry-invariant.

\subsubsection*{Deinterleaving branch}

Every head in the deinterleaving branch rotates by direction instead.
Azimuth is a strong cue for emitter identity, and temporal structure is already present upstream through the \gls{toa}-rotated trunk.
Feeding the raw angle $\theta^{\mathrm{az}}_n$ into \gls{rope} would reintroduce the $0$--$2\pi$ wrap discontinuity of \cref{sec:method:data:norm}, because the relative phase of \cref{eq:method:rope} depends on the algebraic difference of the scalar coordinate.
The branch therefore replaces $\theta^{\mathrm{az}}_n$ by the two continuous features
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:azimuth_trig}
  \tilde{c}_n = \frac{1 + \cos\theta^{\mathrm{az}}_n}{2},
  \qquad
  \tilde{s}_n = \frac{1 + \sin\theta^{\mathrm{az}}_n}{2},
\end{equation}
both in $[0,1]$.
The $d_k/2$ planes of each head are split evenly into two slices of $d_k/4$ planes, in the manner of multi-axis \gls{rope} \parencite{heo2024ropevit}, so that the query (and likewise the key) decomposes as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope_split}
  \vect{q}_n
  =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \vect{q}_n^{(c)} \\
    \vect{q}_n^{(s)}
  \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \vect{q}_n^{(c)},\, \vect{q}_n^{(s)} \in \R^{d_k/2}.
\end{equation}
Each half is rotated by its own single-coordinate matrix of the form \cref{eq:method:rope_block},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope_az}
  \mat{R}^{\mathrm{em}}_n
  =
  \diag\!\bigl(
    \mat{R}^{(c)}(\tilde{c}_n),\;
    \mat{R}^{(s)}(\tilde{s}_n)
  \bigr),
\end{equation}
with $\mat{R}^{(c)},\mat{R}^{(s)}\in\R^{d_k/2\times d_k/2}$ using scales $\gamma_c$ and $\gamma_s$ respectively.
The rotated query is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope_az_q}
  \tilde{\vect{q}}_n
  =
  \mat{R}^{\mathrm{em}}_n\vect{q}_n
  =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \mat{R}^{(c)}(\tilde{c}_n)\,\vect{q}_n^{(c)} \\
    \mat{R}^{(s)}(\tilde{s}_n)\,\vect{q}_n^{(s)}
  \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
and likewise for the key.
Applying \cref{eq:method:rope} slice-wise yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:rope_az_logit}
  \bigl\langle \tilde{\vect{q}}_i,\, \tilde{\vect{k}}_j \bigr\rangle
    = \vect{q}_i^{(c)\top}\,\mat{R}^{(c)}\bigl(\tilde{c}_j - \tilde{c}_i\bigr)\,\vect{k}_j^{(c)}
    + \vect{q}_i^{(s)\top}\,\mat{R}^{(s)}\bigl(\tilde{s}_j - \tilde{s}_i\bigr)\,\vect{k}_j^{(s)}.
\end{equation}
Two bearings just above $0$ and just below $2\pi$ are almost the same direction, but their raw angles differ by nearly a full turn.
Their $(\tilde{c},\tilde{s})$ values are almost the same, so the relative phase in \cref{eq:method:rope_az_logit} stays small.
An algebraic gap in $\theta^{\mathrm{az}}$ itself would not.

The pairwise bias of \cref{eq:method:circ_az} and this rotary encoding can be used separately or combined.
Both the individual variants and their combination are evaluated in \cref{ch:experiments}.
