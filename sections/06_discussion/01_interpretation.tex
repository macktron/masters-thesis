\section{Interpretation of Results}\label{sec:disc:interp}

The tables in \cref{sec:exp:results} say two different things about the two branches.
Mode clustering is already strong with standard self-attention.
Emitter clustering is the place where the attention variants move the score, and they do not all move it the same way.

Vanilla's leftover emitter errors sit in a minority of windows rather than on every window.
The histogram tail in \cref{fig:exp:hist_em} and the off-diagonal mass in \cref{fig:exp:kcorr_em} are the same windows: mixtures where frequency and pulse width are shared across platforms, of the kind drawn in \cref{fig:exp:feat_maxmode}.
Window $100$ is the opposite case. The two emitters are already separated in incidence and in the scanning amplitude, and every model recovers both partitions.

RoPE-TOA puts time into every attention product.
On this corpus that is enough to close the emitter tail, with no extra weights.
RoPE-az spends the deinterleaving rotations on planar incidence instead.
Incidence is a useful input feature, as \cref{fig:exp:feat_w100_em} shows, but using it as the rotary coordinate of the deinterleaving branch does not beat rotating that branch by \gls{toa}.
The embeddings in \cref{fig:exp:emb_w100_em} agree with the table: RoPE-az still splits the two colors, and it does not tighten them the way RoPE-TOA does.

The physical bias reaches the same emitter scores as RoPE-TOA by a more expensive route.
It adds a handful of learned scalars, not a second network.
What it adds at run time is a pairwise map of shape $h \times L \times L$ per layer.
With $h=8$ and $L=2000$, one such map is $128$~MB in fp32.
Twelve attention sublayers make about $1.5$~GB if the maps are all resident, and a training dump at batch size $4$ reports $30$~GB of forward and backward storage for Bias against $19$~GB for Vanilla.
That gap is the pairwise tensors, counted twice when gradients are kept.
At the evaluation batch size of one window the extra is a few gigabytes rather than tens, which matches the encoder time in \cref{tab:exp:efficiency}: $30$~ms instead of $1.5$~ms, while \gls{dbscan} stays near $60$~ms for every model.

RoPE never builds those maps.
Its dumped parameter count matches Vanilla, and its encoder time stays within a couple of milliseconds of Vanilla.
The enormous total-size figure printed for the RoPE summaries is an artifact of expanding rotation blocks in the layer dump, not a measured allocation.

Joint training is a separate axis, taken up in \cref{sec:exp:ablation}.
Emitter-only Vanilla already deinterleaves more cleanly than joint Vanilla, while its untrained mode branch collapses by merging.
The attention variants were all trained jointly, so the emitter gains of RoPE-TOA and Bias are gains under that more demanding objective, not under a deinterleaving-only loss.
