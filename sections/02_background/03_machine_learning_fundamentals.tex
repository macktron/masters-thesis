\section{Machine Learning Fundamentals}\label{sec:bg:ml}

\Gls{ml} replaces hand-coded decision rules with parametric models fitted to data.
A model is a function \(\Enc\colon \setX \to \setY\) from an input space \(\setX\) to a task-specific output space \(\setY\).
Learning searches over parameters \(\bm{\theta}\) so that \(\Enc\) matches the training examples under a chosen loss.
Problems differ in the supervision available, the structure of \(\setY\), and that loss.

The usual training objective is empirical risk minimization.
Given a dataset \(\setD = \{(\vect{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}\) drawn from an unknown distribution and a pointwise loss \(\ell\), the learner solves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bg:erm}
  \hat{\bm{\theta}}
  =
  \argmin_{\bm{\theta}}\;
  \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell\bigl(\Enc(\vect{x}_i),\, y_i\bigr) + \Omega(\bm{\theta}),
\end{equation}
where \(\Omega\) is an optional regularizer that trades fit to the training data against complexity of the fitted model.
Generalization is measured on held-out data.
The training procedure as a whole (data, model class, loss, optimizer, regularization) is judged by that out-of-sample behaviour, not by the training fit.

\subsection*{Learning paradigms}

\Gls{ml} problems are grouped by the supervision available at training time.
In supervised learning, each \(\vect{x}_i\) has a label \(y_i\), categorical for classification or real-valued for regression, and \(\ell\) penalizes disagreement with that label.
In unsupervised learning there are no labels.
The aim is to expose structure in the input distribution, for example by density estimation, dimensionality reduction, or clustering.
\Cref{sec:bg:clustering} treats clustering because this thesis uses it at inference to turn embeddings into emitter and mode partitions.
Self-supervised learning sits between the two: it builds targets from the data itself (masked tokens, contrasted views, shuffled order) so an encoder can be trained without manual annotation.
The remainder of this thesis is supervised.
Training recordings carry per-pulse emitter and operating-mode labels (\cref{sec:method:formulation,sec:method:data}), and the objective uses both streams.
Unsupervised and self-supervised ideas still matter here because the clustering tools in \cref{sec:bg:clustering} and the contrastive machinery in \cref{sec:bg:dl} come from those traditions.

\subsection*{Representation learning}

Much of \gls{dl} trains an encoder that maps observations to a lower-dimensional vector whose geometry is shaped by the loss.
Later steps (classification, clustering, retrieval, sequence prediction) then use the embeddings \(\vect{z} = \Enc(\vect{x}) \in \R^{d}\) rather than the raw coordinates.
Invariances and similarities need only be learned once, in \(\Enc\); simple operations on \(\vect{z}\) often suffice after that.
\Cref{sec:bg:dl,sec:bg:transformer} develop the losses and the encoder.
The method chapter (\cref{ch:method}) applies this to \gls{pdw} streams, with embeddings meant to expose emitter and mode structure.

\subsection*{Applications in radar deinterleaving and mode classification}

Deinterleaving and mode classification are the two tasks this thesis addresses.
For a long time both were handled by temporal rules and hand-crafted statistics on \gls{pdw} parameters (\cref{sec:bg:elint_pipeline}).
As emitters became more agile and environments more crowded, the field moved first to shallow clustering (\(k\)-means and density-based methods over \gls{rf}, \gls{aoa}, and pulse width) and then to deep sequence models that learn pulse-level features from data \parencite{qu2026radardeinterleavingreview}.
Recurrent and attention-based models treat deinterleaving as sequence labelling: the per-pulse output is an emitter.
Transformer encoders can relate pulses that are not neighbours in time, which is useful for jittered, staggered, or grouped \glspl{pri} \parencite{qu2026radardeinterleavingreview,gunn2025deinterleaving}.

Mode classification followed the same path.
Classical \gls{esm} systems match deinterleaved trains to waveform templates in a mode library \parencite{wiley2006elint}.
Learning-based work treats the task as supervised classification over known modes, or as unsupervised grouping of trains when labels are scarce \parencite{qu2026radardeinterleavingreview}.
This thesis uses per-pulse mode labels from a global catalogue at training time, but the loss is supervised contrastive rather than a closed-catalogue softmax.
The emitter term is the same kind of contrastive signal, with labels that are local to each recording.
Inference on both branches is per-window clustering.
\Cref{sec:bg:clustering,sec:bg:dl,sec:bg:transformer} supply the tools the method chapter uses.
