\section{Datasets}\label{sec:exp:datasets}

All experiments in this chapter use synthetic \gls{pdw} streams generated by the proprietary scenario simulator introduced in \cref{sec:method:data}.
The simulator supplies time-ordered pulse records with ground-truth emitter and mode labels.
Those labels supervise the losses in \cref{sec:method:loss} and the scores in \cref{sec:method:metrics,sec:exp:protocol}.
Mode labels are drawn from a catalogue shared across the three splits.

A scenario is one simulated timeline of \glspl{pdw}.
Scenarios are assigned to training, validation, and test splits before windowing.
Carrier frequency, pulse width, and log-amplitude are standardized with training-split statistics (\cref{sec:method:data}).
\Gls{toa} is min--max mapped inside each window.

The encoder sees windows of $L = 2000$ pulses.
Training uses stride $\delta = 200$, so consecutive training windows overlap by $1800$ pulses.
Validation and test windows use $\delta = L$ and do not overlap.

Those two columns of \cref{tab:exp:datasets} are not the same kind of count.
The pulse column is the number of uniquely generated \glspl{pdw} in the split.
The window column is the number of encoder inputs after the stride of \cref{sec:method:data:window}.
Training contains about six times as many unique pulses as validation or test, in line with the scenario counts.
The training stride is $L/10$, so each training pulse is reused in about ten overlapping windows and the window count is about fifty times that of validation or test.
That extra factor is overlap, not additional unique pulses.

The largest number of distinct emitters in a window is $7$ in every split.
The largest number of distinct modes is $17$ in training, $15$ in validation, and $19$ in test.
A window is a slice of a scenario, so these peaks are what the cut happened to contain, not a constraint imposed on the windows.

Pulse counts per emitter and per mode are unbalanced inside windows.
Scanning modes produce far fewer pulses than lock-on modes, and some modes differ by more than an order of magnitude in base \gls{pri}.
